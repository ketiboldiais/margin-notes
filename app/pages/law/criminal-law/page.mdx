## General Concepts in Criminal Law
1. __Criminal liability__ is liability arising from the commission of a crime.
2. A __crime__ is an act or omission prohibited by law for the protection of the public, the violation of which is prosecuted by the state in its own name, and punishable by fine, incarceration, other restrictions upon liberty, or some combination of these.[^mpc_104(1)]
3. Sources of Criminal Law
    1. __English Common Law__. Definitions of basic crimes and defenses were developed in the English courts and early American courts adopted those decisions. Although most criminal law issues today are governed by statute, statutory language is often interpreted according to English common law.
    2. __Statutory Law__. Criminal law matters are now largely governed by federal or state statutes. Most criminal law matters (about ${98\percent}$) are dealt with at the state level.[^ncsc_criminal_law]
    3. __Model Penal Code__. The Model Penal Code is a proposed penal code developed and approved by the American Law Institute. The Code is not "law," since the ALI has no authority to promulgate law, but the Code provides a basis for many state criminal statutes.

[^mpc_104(1)]: Model Penal Code § ${1.04\lpar 1 \rpar}$.
[^ncsc_criminal_law]: Nat’l Ctr. for State Cts., _Timely Justice in Criminal Cases: What the Data Tells Us_ 2 (2024).

## Classifying Crimes
### Common Law Classification of Crimes
1. Traditionally, crimes were divided into three different categories: `(`1`)` felonies, `(`2`)` misdemeanors, and `(`3`)` treason.
    1. __Felonies__: Offenses punishable by total forfeiture of land, goods, or both.
    2. __Misdemeanor__: Any offense other than a felony or treason.
    3. __Treason__: An offense of betraying the sovereign or the state, or more broadly, breaching allegiance.[^blackstone_4] Historically, English common law distinguished between high treason and petty treason.[^blackstone_treason]
       1. __High treason__: Treason directed against a sovereign.[^blackstone_treason_1]
       2. __Petty treason__: The killing of a superior to whom the offender owed a special duty of allegiance.[^blackstone_treason_2] 

### Malum in Se and Malum Prohibitum
1. Crimes may also be classified as __malum in se__ or __malum prohibitum__.
    1. A crime is __malum in se__ when the conduct in question is wrong because it is considered inherently wrongful or immoral, regardless of whether the law prohibits the act.[^malum_in_se]
    2. A crime is __malum in prohibitum__ when the conduct in question is wrong because the law prohibits the act.[^malum_in_prohibitum]

## Constitutional Restrictions
### Burden of Proof
1. The prosecution has the burden of proof in a criminal proceeding.[^in_re_winship]
2. The prosecution must prove all elements of the crime __beyond a reasonable doubt__.[^in_re_winship_2]  

<aside>
The prosecution bears a high burden of proof because of the risk of convicting an innocent person.
</aside>

[^in_re_winship]: _In re Winship_, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970).
[^in_re_winship_2]: _Id_.

### Right to Trial by Jury
1. A criminal defendant has a right to a jury trial in both state and federal court.[^right_to_trial]
2. In federal criminal cases, a jury typically consists of ${12}$ jurors, but the Constitution does not require a ${12}$-person jury in every criminal case.[^jury_count_fed]
   1. The U.S. Supreme Court has held, however, that a ${6}$ person jury can satisfy the Sixth Amendment in a state criminal case.[^williams_v_florida]
   2. A ${5}$ person jury, however, is too small for a state criminal case.[^ballew_v_georgia]
3. State criminal cases ordinarily use ${12}$ jurors, but the exact number will vary depending on the criminal case.[^state_statutes_for_jury_count]

### Jury Consideration and Determination
1. Treating an element of a crime as a matter of law and withdrawing it from jury consideration violates the defendant's right to due process and a jury trial.[^gaudin]

    <details>
    <summary>Case Study: _United States v. Gaudin_, 515 U.S. 506 (1995)</summary>
    <section>
    __~facts~__. The federal government prosecuted Louis Gaudin under 18 U.S.C. § 1001 for making materially false statements on federal loan documents. The U.S. alleged that Gaudin inflated property values in documents submitted to the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD). Under 18 U.S.C. § 1001, a materially false statement is a false statement important or relevant enough to influence, or is capable of influencing, the decision or function of the person or government agency receiving it. At trial, the judge told the jury that whether Gaudin's false statements were "material" was a question for the court, not the jury. The judge determined that the statements were material and effectively instructed the jury that this element had been established. 

    __~question~__. Can a judge remove an element of a criminal offense—materiality—from the jury and decide it as a matter of law?

    __~answer~__. No. The Constitution gives a criminal defendant the right to demand that a jury find him guilty of all the elements of the crime with which he is charged.
    </section>
    </details>

[^gaudin]: _United States v. Gaudin_, 515 U.S. 506, 509–10, 522–23 (1995).

2. The Sixth Amendment's Due Process Clause, notice requirement, and jury trial guarantee give a defendant a right to have the jury determine facts that increase the maximum and minimum sentence which can be imposed upon conviction.[^apprendi]
   1. __Exception: Prior Conviction Increasing Possible Sentence__. The right to have a jury determination, however, does not apply to a prior conviction relied upon by the prosecution to increase the sentence applicable upon conviction.[^almendarez]
   2. __Exception: Facts Affecting Judicial Discretion in Sentencing__. A defendant has no right to have a jury decide facts that considered by a judge exercising discretion on the length of the sentence within the statutorily-described minimum and maximum.[^obrien]

## Burdens on the Defendant
1. The __burden of production__ is a party's obligation to present enough evidence on an issue so that the factfinder can consider and decide that issue.[^blacks_bop]  
2. Generally, for affirmative defenses, a criminal defendant bears the burden of production.[^hartstock]
    1. __Self-defense__. Ordinarily, the defendant must introduce sufficient evidence supporting the elements of self-defense before the factfinder can receive a self-defense instruction.[^engel] 
    2. __Duress__. In federal criminal cases, the defendant bears the burden of establishing the factual basis for duress.[^dixon] 
    3. __Necessity__. Before a criminal defendant can submit the defense of necessity to a jury, the defendant must present evidence that could support a finding in his favor for each element of the defense.[^unser]
    4. __Entrapment__. The defendant must present evidence of government inducement, after which the prosecution bears the burden of proving the defendant's predisposition to commit the crime.[^matthews] 
3. The __burden of persuasion__ is a party's obligation to convince the factfinder that a fact has been established within the certainty required by the applicable standard of proof.[^microsoft]  
4. For some defenses, a criminal defendant bears the burden of persuasion.[^patterson]
    1. __Insanity__. Under federal law, the defendant bears the burden of persuasion and the burden of production for the defense of insanity—the defendant must establish insanity by clear and convincing evidence.[^18_usc_17b]

## Presumptions Shifting Burden of Proof
1. When an element of a criminal offense is disputed, a jury instruction violates the Due Process Clause if a reasonable juror could interpret it as creating a conclusive presumption or shifting the burden of persuasion on that element to the defendant.[^sandstrom]
    <details>
    <summary>Case Study: _Sandstrom v. Montana_, 442 U.S. 510 (1979)</summary>
    <section>
    __~facts~__. David Sandstrom confessed to killing Annie Jessen. Montana charged him with deliberate homicide. Deliberate homicide required the state to prove that he purposely or knowingly caused Jessen's death. Sandstrom admitted that he killed Jessen but disputed the required mental state. Over Sandstrom's objection, the trial judge instructed the jury: "The law presumes that a person intends the ordinary consequences of his voluntary acts." The jury convicted Sandstrom. Sandstrom appealed, arguing that the judge's instruction impermissibly shifted the burden of proving intent from the prosecution to him. The Montana Supreme Court affirmed. Sandstrom petitioned the U.S. Supreme Court. The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed.    

    __~issue~__. Does a jury instruction stating that "the law presumes that a person intends the ordinary consquences of his voluntary acts" violate the Fourteenth Amendment when intent is an element of the charged offense?

    __~holding~__. Yes. The instruction violated the Due Process Clause because a reasonable juror could have interpreted the instruction as a conclusive presumption of intent or as a mandatory presumption that shifted the burden of persuasion on intent to the defendant. Either interpretation impermissibly relieved the prosectuion of its constitutional obligation to prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.
    </section>
    </details>

## Permissive Inferences
1. A __permissive inference__ is an inference that a factfinder is permitted—but not required—to make, based on proof of another fact.[^ulster]
    1. ~example~. Jury Instruction: "If you find that the defendant routinely deals with stolen property, you may infer that the defendant knew that the property in this case was stolen."  
2. A permissive inference does not violate due process if the ultimate fact (the fact to be inferred by the factfinder) more likely than not flows from the fact prosecution proved.[^ulster_2] 


[^ulster]: _See_ _County Court of Ulster County v. Allen_, 442 U.S. 140, 157 (1979).
